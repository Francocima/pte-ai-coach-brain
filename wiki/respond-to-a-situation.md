# Respond to a Situation — Technique

**Summary**: Strategy for the Respond to a Situation speaking task added in August 2025 — Pearson's situation/person/goal framework, the SIMPLE structure, and what relevant-and-sufficient Content means.

**Sources**: `PTE Respond to a Situation Strategy 90 Score Method.md` (Sandeep / Best PTE Coaching, YouTube transcript), `Prepare for PTE Academic _ Respond to a situation _ Everything you need to know 💡.md` (Pearson, official), `PTE-Academic-Test-Taker-Score-Guide.pdf` (Pearson, official)

**Last updated**: 2026-06-04

---

## Task at a glance

- A real-life campus scenario is shown as text **and** read aloud as audio (same words)
- After the audio: **10 seconds** to prepare, then **40 seconds** to respond
- **2–3 questions** per exam
- Skills assessed: **Speaking only** (PTE Academic)
- Comes **last** in the speaking module (after Read Aloud, Repeat Sentence, Describe Image, Retell Lecture, Answer Short Question, SGD) (source: Sandeep)
- **New task — added to PTE Academic in August 2025.** It existed in PTE Core before but is new to Academic (source: Sandeep)

## Official scoring (Pearson Score Guide)

| Trait | Max |
|-------|-----|
| Content | 6 |
| Pronunciation | 5 |
| Oral Fluency | 5 |

(source: PTE-Academic-Test-Taker-Score-Guide.pdf, pp. 29–31)

Content is **human-reviewed**, and **memorised responses score 0 for Content** (which zeroes the whole question). See [[pte-score-guide]].

## Pearson's framework: decode the description first

The task description hands you three things; read for all three (source: Pearson):
1. **Situation** — what's happening (the context).
2. **Person** — who you're speaking to (sets the tone/formality: peer vs. professor vs. admin).
3. **Goal** — what you must achieve in the conversation.

Example: *"You're working on an essay and having difficulty organising your ideas. You approach your professor after class and ask if you could come to her office to discuss your essay."*
- Situation: difficulty organising essay ideas.
- Person: your professor → polite, formal-ish.
- Goal: request an office meeting to discuss the essay.

Every added detail must stay **consistent with the prompt**. If it says "ask to come to her office," don't say "can I meet you in the library" — that drops Content (source: Pearson).

## The SIMPLE structure (Sandeep)

A six-step order so every answer is consistent under pressure. **S-I-M-P-L-E**:

| Letter | Step | Example |
|--------|------|---------|
| **S** | **Greeting** — start the conversation | "Hello professor, how are you?" |
| **I** | **Introduce** the topic | "I'm working on the essay for our course…" |
| **M** | **Mention** the problem | "…but I'm struggling to organise my ideas." |
| **P** | **Politely** ask/request | "Could you please allow me to visit your office to discuss it?" |
| **L** | **Lay** an option (optional) | "I could come tomorrow morning or any time that suits you." |
| **E** | **End** it | "Thank you so much in advance, professor." |

**L is optional** — use it if a natural alternative/option fits the moment, skip it otherwise. For a peer, you can stay relaxed but still polite; no need to change your language completely (source: Sandeep).

The value of a structure is **consistency**: with a process, fluency and content quality stay stable across all 2–3 questions. Without one, they fluctuate (source: Sandeep).

## What scores well on Content

The official requirement is **relevant AND sufficient** (source: Pearson + Sandeep):
- **Relevant:** address the actual problem. If the prompt is about organising an essay, talk about *that* — not "my project."
- **Sufficient:** enough detail to accomplish the goal. "I have a problem with my essay, can you help?" is relevant but **not sufficient** — it doesn't state the request clearly enough.
- The top band (6) "expands beyond the prompt language to provide a persuasive response" — so reformulate, don't just repeat the prompt's words.

Language tips (source: Pearson):
- Speak in the **first person** (I, not he/she) — you are the person in the situation.
- Use **modal verbs** for politeness: *would I be able to…, could I…, may I…*
- Natural contractions (I'm, I'd) sound authentic and help fluency.
- Make the request **early**, and consider repeating it at the end.

## Delivery

- You **don't need the full 40 seconds** — ~30–35s covers it if every element is present (source: Sandeep).
- Some fillers/pauses are natural for an impromptu task; they fade with practice. Don't let silence hit 3 seconds.
- Pronunciation = clarity, not accent. Stress multi-syllable words cleanly (e.g. "batchmates") (source: Sandeep).

## Worked example

Prompt: *you booked a library meeting room 2–3pm for a group project; it's just after 2pm and other students are sitting in it.*

> "Hi there. I booked this meeting room from 2 to 3pm to work on a group project with my classmates. It's already past 2 and they'll be arriving shortly, so could you please vacate the room? There are several other free rooms in the same building you're welcome to use. Thanks so much for understanding."

That hits greeting, claim/topic, problem, polite request, an option, and a close — relevant and sufficient.

## More worked examples

### Example 2 — Expressing interest in a position

Prompt: *Your tutor is looking for volunteers to help prepare for the school Open Week. You are interested in this position and want to tell him why you are suitable for it.*

> "Hi! I heard you're looking for volunteers to help prepare for Open Week, and I'd really like to be involved. I have strong organisational skills and I'm fully available during the preparation period, so I could help coordinate activities or set up for the event. I'd love the opportunity to contribute. Please let me know how I can help."

**SIMPLE breakdown:** Greet (Hi) → Introduce topic (Open Week volunteering) → no explicit problem, so move straight to suitability → Politely offer (I'd really like to be involved) → Lay option (coordinate or set up) → End (let me know how I can help).

**Common template trap to avoid:** Opening with "I would like to discuss an issue we have encountered with volunteering for Open Week" is flagged as a memorised response and misframes the situation — expressing interest is not an issue. Phrases like "The background is that…" and "In the situation, what I can think of is…" are rigid template markers that trigger human review.

### Example 3 — Making a request from staff

Prompt: *You left your jacket at the library and didn't realise it until you got home. You need to send a message to the library staff asking if they can keep it before you go back tomorrow.*

> "Hello, I was at the library earlier today and I think I left my jacket behind. I only noticed when I got home, so I wasn't able to come back in time. Could you please check if it's been found and set it aside for me? I'll come to collect it tomorrow morning. Thank you so much for your help."

**SIMPLE breakdown:** Greet (Hello) → Introduce topic (was at the library today) → Mention problem (left jacket, only noticed at home) → Politely ask (could you check and set it aside) → Lay option (I'll collect it tomorrow morning) → End (thank you).

# Describe Image — Technique

**Summary**: Strategy for the Describe Image speaking task — the official do's and don'ts, why memorised templates now score 0, and the detailed Content rubric.

## Task at a glance

- An image (bar graph, line graph, map, pie chart, process, photo) appears on screen
- **25 seconds** to prepare, then speak for up to **40 seconds**
- **5–6 questions** per exam
- Skills assessed: **Speaking only**

## Official scoring (Pearson Score Guide)

| Trait | Max |
|-------|-----|
| Content | 6 |
| Pronunciation | 5 |
| Oral Fluency | 5 |

(source: PTE-Academic-Test-Taker-Score-Guide.pdf, pp. 18–20)

**Content is human-reviewed.** If Content scores 0, Pronunciation and Oral Fluency are **not scored** and the question is worth nothing. See [[pte-score-guide]].

## The critical rule: memorised templates score 0

Pearson's official article states it plainly: **do not use memorised responses or templated sentences. Such responses can lead to a score of 0 for content — and if that happens, your fluency and pronunciation will not be scored either** (source: Pearson, do's and don'ts article).

This sits in tension with the popular coaching template (e.g. "The image provided is a [graph] that gives information about… The X-axis talks about… It is evident that…"). The resolution:
- A rigid sentence-by-sentence template applied identically to every image is the risk — it reads as pre-prepared and can be flagged.
- A **light frame** filled with the **actual specific data** of the image is safe.
- The deciding factor is whether the words describe *this* image. Generic scaffolding with no real image content is what scores 0.

> Treat the template in [[speaking-modules]] as a loose frame to fill with real observations, not a script to recite verbatim.

## Official do's (Pearson)

1. **Use prep time to find the key trend.** In 25 seconds, identify the key trends and the single most significant one. Focus your response there.
2. **Speak 30–40 seconds.** Ideal for covering key features without rushing or running dry.
3. **Incorporate numbers.** Cite specific data points from the image — it makes the description precise and concrete.

## Official don'ts (Pearson)

1. **No memorised/templated responses** (see above — the 0-Content trap).
2. **Don't try to mention every feature.** Prioritise the most significant trends and data points within 40 seconds.
3. **Don't dwell on labels.** Mention them, but spend your time on the *trends and data*, not on reading axis labels.
4. **No personal opinions.** Describe only what's visible.

## What the Content rubric rewards

The official Content scale shows exactly what separates bands (source: Score Guide, pp. 18–19):
- **6:** describes the image fully and accurately, **expands on relationships between features** for a nuanced interpretation; a listener could build a complete mental picture.
- **3:** "mainly superficial descriptions… narrow vocabulary… a listener could visualise elements, but not a cohesive whole."
- **1:** "disconnected elements, or a list of points identified on the image, without description or elaboration."

The lesson: **relationships and trends** lift you to 5–6; a **list of disconnected labels** drops you to 1. Same principle as the keyword-dump failure in [[retell-lecture]].

## A safe spoken shape

Frame → fill with real data → relate → close:

> "This bar chart compares smartphone sales for four brands from 2020 to 2023. Samsung leads throughout, rising to around 40 million units in 2023, while Nokia declines steadily to under 5 million. Apple and Xiaomi sit in between, with Xiaomi showing the fastest growth. Overall, the data points to newer brands gaining dominance as established ones lose share."

Numbers cited, the significant trend named, a relationship drawn, no opinion, ~35 seconds.

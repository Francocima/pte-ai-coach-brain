# Summarize Spoken Text — Technique

**Summary**: Complete strategy for the SST listening task — official scoring, the "meaningful sentence" rule that the AI now enforces, the three-phase method, and template scaffolding.

**Sources**: `Best Way to Master Summarize Spoken Test in PTE Listening.md` (Language Academy), `Summarise Spoken Text Masterplan New Template_.md` (Skills PTE Academic / Nakul, YouTube transcript), `The Latest Summarize Spoken Text Templates (2026).md` (PTE Tutoring Online), `PTE Summarize Written Text Guide 2026.md` (OneAustraliaGroup — SST word-limit section), `PTE-Academic-Test-Taker-Score-Guide.pdf` (Pearson, official)

**Last updated**: 2026-06-04

## Task at a glance

- Listen to a 60–90 second audio clip (lecture or interview), played **once**
- Write a summary of **50–70 words** (PTE Academic) / 20–30 words (PTE Core)
- 10 minutes total, **including the audio**
- **1 question** per exam (occasionally 2) (source: Skills PTE / Nakul)
- Skills assessed: **Listening AND Writing** — it feeds both scores, so it is high-value
- If the timer expires mid-typing, whatever you have written is saved automatically (source: Skills PTE / Nakul)

## Official scoring (Pearson Score Guide)

| Trait | Max | Detail |
|-------|-----|--------|
| Content | 4 | Captures main ideas, synthesised and reformulated |
| Form | 2 | 50–70 words = 2 |
| Grammar | 2 | 2 = correct; 1 = errors but no hindrance; 0 = defective |
| Vocabulary | 2 | 2 = appropriate; 1 = lexical errors, no hindrance; 0 = defective |
| Spelling | 2 | 2 = correct; 1 = one error; 0 = more than one error |
| **Total** | **12** | |

(source: PTE-Academic-Test-Taker-Score-Guide.pdf, pp. 41–43)

**Note on Content:** SST also receives a **human review of Content** before the final score is set — it is one of seven task types double-scored this way (source: Score Guide, p. 15). See [[pte-score-guide]].

> ⚠️ **Coaching contradiction:** Nakul's video states Content is worth 2 marks; the official guide and Language Academy both say **4**. Trust the official figure. Content is the highest-weighted trait.

## Form: the official limit is softer than coaches say

The official Form rubric awards **partial credit** outside 50–70:

- 50–70 words → **2** (full)
- 40–49 or 71–100 words → **1** (partial)
- under 40 or over 100 words → **0**

(source: Score Guide, p. 42)

Most coaches teach a hard "50–70 or you get zero" rule. That is a deliberate safety simplification, not the literal rule. **Still aim for 55–65 words** — being inside the full-credit band protects two marks with zero downside.

## The "meaningful sentence" rule (this is the one that bites)

The single most important shift: the AI and human reviewer now check the **meaning** of your sentences, not just the presence of keywords. Sentences assembled from a keyword template — "X, Y, and Z were highlighted; A, B, and C were mentioned" — score in the lowest Content bands because they are **disconnected excerpts without context** (Score Guide Content score 1: "composed of disconnected ideas or excerpts... without any context or attempt at synthesis").

The fix (source: Skills PTE / Nakul):

- Write **phrases and short sentences, not keywords**. Four to five real phrases beat a comma-separated keyword list.
- A short genuine phrase proves you understood the audio. A keyword slot-fill does not.
- It is psychologically tempting to hunt for the "important" keyword. There is no bonus for important words — an average idea written as a proper sentence scores the same Content as a "key" idea, and far higher than a keyword. Spend your effort on **grammar and spelling**, not on word-hunting.

This directly explains the common exam comment *"Do not write meaningless sentences using a keyword template — no scores are awarded for meaningless sentences."* The cause is almost always a template filled with nouns instead of summarised meaning, and additive connectors ("not only… but also") stacked without a controlling idea.

## Grammar and spelling decide the score

You can have all the content from the audio, but six grammar errors and ten spelling mistakes still drop you to near zero on those traits (source: Skills PTE / Nakul). Because Grammar (2) + Spelling (2) + Vocabulary (2) = 6 of the 12 marks sit on mechanical accuracy, **English accuracy outweighs content coverage** at the margin.

Unlike Retell Lecture, **you may freely add, delete, or alter words** to make the template and your captured phrases fit grammatically. Changing verb forms, inserting articles, or adding words not in the audio is all fine as long as the result is grammatical and on-topic (source: Skills PTE / Nakul).

## The three-phase method (Language Academy)

**Phase 1 — Active listening (60–90s):**
- Lock onto the first 10–15 seconds: the speaker states the topic there. Write 2–3 keywords from it.
- Flag transition phrases ("however," "the main reason," "most importantly," "in conclusion") — they signal emphasised points.
- Take structured shorthand notes (govt, ↑, →, b/c), not a transcript.
- Identify 3–4 main points maximum. Don't panic if you miss something.

**Phase 2 — Plan (1–2 min):** Review notes, identify the main topic and 2–3 supporting points, decide how you'll connect them.

**Phase 3 — Write and review (5–6 min write, 2–3 min review):**
- Open with "The lecture discusses…" / "The speaker explains…"
- Paraphrase; keep only technical terms and proper nouns verbatim.
- Review order: **word count first**, then spelling, then grammar, then read it back for sense.

For the listen-write note rhythm (5s listen / 5s write alternation), see [[note-taking]].

## Template as scaffolding (not as content)

A short template gives structure; it earns no marks itself, so keep it minimal (it should consume ~10 of your 70 words, not 50). Two interchangeable options (source: Skills PTE / Nakul):

Old SST template:
> "The talk was about ___. Firstly it was clear that ___. Later we learned that ___. Furthermore it represented the fact that ___. In the end the lecture highlighted that ___."

Reused Retell Lecture template (also valid for SST):
> "The lecture discusses ___. In the beginning the speaker explained ___. Later the speaker said ___. Here the speaker also mentioned ___. In conclusion the lecture effectively summarised ___."

Use the **same** template every time, even across two questions. Fill the blanks with reformulated phrases, then edit for grammar.

## Template safety warning (PTE Tutoring Online)

Pearson penalises responses that look generic or mechanically structured. A template that could apply to *any* lecture is a liability. Keep the scaffolding light and make the filled content unmistakably tied to the specific audio. The structure is a flexible frame, not a memorised sentence bank. (source: The Latest Summarize Spoken Text Templates 2026)

## Adapt the frame to the audio type (PTE Tutoring Online)

- **Cause-and-effect:** "The speaker explained that ___ leads to ___."
- **Comparison:** "___ differs from ___ in terms of ___, while both share ___."
- **Problem-solution:** "The speaker identified challenges like ___, then proposed ___."
- **Informational:** main topic + 2–3 supporting points + one-line takeaway.

## Common mistakes

- Keyword templates / meaningless sentences (lowest Content band — the core failure)
- Missing the word count (check it first in review)
- Cramming every minor detail and running out of words before the main idea
- Copying the audio verbatim (hurts Vocabulary; also can't always be grammatically fitted)
- Spelling errors (mixing British/American — pick one and stay consistent; if unsure of a word, use a simpler synonym you can spell)
- Spending 8–9 minutes writing with no time to review
